Agents with their own computers

A first-principles teardown of Meta Muse, OpenAI dots and Grok Bot: every feature mapped to one vocabulary, a high-level and low-level design for each, and the decisions worth copying if you are building one yourself.

Research current to 25 November 2026 53 sources, primary documentation first About an hour to read; tables are for scanning

1Key findings

Between August and November 2026 the three largest consumer AI platforms shipped the same idea: an agent that keeps working when you close the app, on a computer of its own, with your accounts plugged in. The idea is the same. The engineering bets underneath it are very different, and they are what matter if you plan to build something similar.

  1. The product is the computer plus the gatekeeper, not the chatbot. Each product gives the agent a persistent cloud machine with a real browser, a shell and files, and routes outward-bound actions through a separate checker: Muse's Sentinel, dots' Auto-review, Grok Bot's Auto Review.21739
  2. They disagree about where trust is enforced. Meta put it in the operating system: a systemd-nspawn cell, eBPF-based taint tracking and surrogate tokens swapped in at the network edge.2 OpenAI put it in judgment: a trained model, a separate reviewer agent and live safety monitoring on top of a sandbox.1726 SpaceXSI, operating Grok Bot on Cursor's infrastructure, put it in administration: network policy, inference hooks and audit pipelines. Inside one user's account, every Bot shares a single machine.3940
  3. Proactivity is the new frontier, and each product fences it differently. Dots run "proactive research" with tools that are read-only, enforced in code.17 Grok Bot's primary Bot offers work first and acts only after you say yes.47 Muse messages you unprompted, but only when something clears what Meta calls a high bar.3
  4. Nobody lets the model see your passwords, but payment safety varies a lot. Muse issues a single-use card tied to one merchant, amount and time window, and approves every purchase.2 Dots need your approval for purchases and biometrics for each Wallet payment.1720 Grok Bot fills a card saved in its Vault at checkout without asking, and "no setting requires your approval for each use." In app chats the Bot can read that card's number and security code, and one Pay approval on an order card can cover up to 500 payments for 24 hours.5339
  5. Approval fatigue is the central design problem. OpenAI measured that letting a reviewer agent decide escalations cut human interruptions about 200-fold, while catching 90 to 99 percent of the risky cases in its evaluations.26 Meta's designers name "banner blindness" as the failure mode to avoid.3 Grok Bot's changelog is a running record of removing approval cards that did not need to exist.36
  6. Each one bets on a different distribution moat. Muse rides WhatsApp and Meta's apps and is writing a protocol for businesses (Poppy).16 Dots ride ChatGPT's plugin ecosystem and enterprise contracts.1624 Grok Bot rides Slack, Teams and meetings for whole teams, with Cursor's enterprise controls.3441
  7. All three admit prompt injection is unsolved. Meta calls it "an open problem in the industry," OpenAI says Auto-review "is not a deterministic security guarantee," and Grok Bot's docs say its controls "reduce, but do not eliminate" the risk.22539 Treat every architecture here as damage limitation, not prevention.

4.3 Combinations that matter

Fifty-six features have more pairings than anyone could test, but almost all the risk and value comes from six of them. Each is a pairing that no single feature produces on its own, and each product's answer to it tells you more about the architecture than any feature list.

Private data, untrusted content, and a way out

Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" is the starting point for all three designs: an agent that can read your private data, is exposed to text an attacker controls, and can communicate externally can be tricked into leaking your data.15 Meta quotes it at length and says the problem "has been an obsession for us."2 An always-on agent with your inbox and a browser has all three legs by default, so each design is a choice of which leg to cut, and when.

Muse

Cuts the exit. Each tool process starts "clean" and is marked tainted once it reads user data. A tainted process loses auto-allow, so its network requests fall back to the normal approval flow.2

dots

Cuts at the action. Auto-review checks each write (who receives it, what it contains) against your instructions, and the more sensitive the data, the more specific the recipient must be.17

Grok Bot

Cuts at action and network. Auto Review checks actions, an Enterprise network policy limits destinations, and inference hooks let your own server screen what reaches the model.39

Always on, unprompted, and able to write

An agent that works while you sleep and also takes initiative can do damage nobody asked for and nobody is watching. Dots draw the sharpest line: proactive research runs only with read-only tools, enforced in code. It "cannot directly send messages to other people, change content in connected apps, or control a browser or desktop."17 Grok Bot makes initiative an offer, and acts only after you accept.47 Muse sets a bar on interruption rather than on capability: a proactive message "has to be genuinely helpful and worth the interruption," and you can dial it up or down.3

A browser, live sessions, and credentials

Once an agent signs in through a real browser, the session cookie becomes the credential, and the model can read whatever is on the page. Muse's browser sub-agent sees only an accessibility-tree snapshot, never the raw DOM. It cannot run JavaScript, Chrome DevTools are disabled, and the agent is paused while credentials are filled.2 OpenAI warns enterprise admins that "code on the dot's computer can read an app token or browser cookie if its value is exposed to that code; do not assume the session or token is isolated."23 In Grok Bot, a sign-in by one Bot signs in every Bot on your account, because all of them share the browser.38

Payments without a human at the checkout

This combination separates the three most clearly. Muse detects a checkout page, prompts with the exact purchase, and pays with a single-use card valid for one merchant, one amount and a limited time, "even if it were stolen via prompt injection or some other attack, it would not be particularly useful to the attacker."2 Dots need approval for purchases, take Wallet payments only with biometrics, and hand transfers between financial accounts back to you.1720 Grok Bot gives you the most convenience and the least friction: a card saved in its Vault fills without per-use approval, and one Pay tap on an order card covers up to 500 payments for 24 hours.53

Many channels, one memory, different audiences

The same agent in your private chat, a team channel and a meeting will know things one audience shouldn't hear. OpenAI states the principle most cleanly: a dot "can use a deadline you shared privately to help you prepare an update, but sharing private details in a team channel still requires permission."19 Grok Bot enforces something similar per post, checking "each post against who can read it" in shared Slack conversations, and its meeting Bot won't share your credentials in a meeting.3646

Several agents, one machine

Multi-agent systems raise a quiet question: is the boundary between agents a security boundary? For Grok Bot the documented answer is no. Files, browser sessions and command-line credentials are shared by every Bot on the account, and the docs advise: "Treat anything on the computer as available to all your Bots."3738 Dots give each delegated task "its own conversation," without automatic access to everything the dot knows.19 Muse's sub-agents run as part of the harness, inside the one runtime cell that is contained as a whole.2

5Meta Muse: security enforced by the operating system

Meta's bet is that a personal agent for billions of people cannot depend on the model behaving well, so the guarantees live in Linux. The model proposes; containers, sockets, eBPF programs and a host-side Sentinel decide what actually happens.

In Meta's codebase, Muse is called "Hatch."2 The first line of its system prompt is "Your purpose is to make your user's life better,"3 and the product is designed around one long conversation, "the kind you'd have with another person," rather than turn-by-turn chat. You can interrupt it, pile up several requests at once, and open side chats when a project needs its own context. Because Muse can message you out of turn, the transcript is broken into chat bubbles that "mark where one thought ends and the next begins."3 Everything below serves that experience: an agent that keeps working in the background, connects to your accounts one at a time, and comes back only when "something is meaningfully new or needs your input."3

5.1 High-level design

The unit of deployment is a person, not a request. Each person gets a dedicated Linux VM with "enough storage, CPU, and memory to do real work," and that VM is "the system of record for everything you put in Muse."2 The apps "connect directly to your VM via a secure transport layer."2 Data leaves the VM only through constrained paths: proxies for inference and telemetry, and network egress that Sentinel governs. Separately, the VM "is backed up continuously so you can restore it if something goes wrong."2

5.2 Low-level design

The runtime cell

The cell is a systemd-nspawn container. Root inside it maps to an unprivileged host user, "so runtime cell root is not host root." It gets its own root filesystem (a full Debian image), a virtual network interface, a system-call filter (for example, no io_uring), and a reduced set of kernel capabilities (no CAP_SYS_PTRACE, no CAP_NET_ADMIN).2 The harness, your files and every tool the agent runs live here. That includes code the agent writes for itself: Meta's team says Muse "launches swarms of subagents, builds its own tools, and edits itself."2 The cell is designed on the assumption that whatever runs inside it may already be hostile.

Connector calls: three separate deciders

The usual way to give an agent app access is a command-line tool holding a token, running right next to the agent. Muse rejects that design because "the agent could be coerced to change the tool code via prompt injection."2 Built-in connectors are split in two. A thin CLI inside the cell only parses arguments, opens files the caller can already read, and passes typed arguments and file descriptors over a Unix socket. A systemd-sandboxed worker outside the cell runs the actual logic. Each worker is identified by its cgroup and has an explicit credential allowlist, so "a calendar worker cannot ask authd for an email credential simply by changing a request parameter." Three components decide independently:2

  • privsep decides where credential-capable code runs;
  • authd decides which credential the authenticated caller may receive;
  • Sentinel decides whether the action may happen at all.

For each connector call, the tool submits to Sentinel the connector, the method, the class of action, its scope and the context of the user's request. Sentinel turns that into a "user-visible purpose," checks it against the policy the user set, and returns allow, deny or ask.2 Where a service lets you, Muse splits read and write access, and it adds controls finer than OAuth scopes. Grant Gmail read access, for example, and you can still remove access to Gmail settings, which normally comes bundled with it.2

Egress, surrogate tokens and SSRF

Every network request from the cell goes through a forward proxy, enforced with user namespaces, veth boundaries and eBPF filtering, and Sentinel evaluates it at layer 4 and layer 7: "the hostname, the resolved and final destination IP address, the port, protocol, HTTP method, path, and the actual decoded request." SSRF checks stop a public-looking hostname from resolving to private infrastructure after DNS lookup.2 Code in the cell never holds a real secret. It gets a surrogate token minted by authd, and Sentinel swaps in the real credential at the network boundary only after the request has been authorized. "Any attempt to coerce the agent to reveal the actual secrets via prompt-injection or otherwise is futile," because the agent never had them.2

Tainted egress: fewer prompts without blind spots

Asking about every request would bury users in prompts. Never asking would turn the agent into an exfiltration channel. Muse resolves this with kernel-level data-flow tracking that Meta calls "tainted egress." Every tool process starts clean and becomes tainted once it reads user data. A clean request that already fits a "narrowly bounded auto allow policy," and passes the other URL checks, goes out without a prompt. A tainted or unverifiable process loses auto-allow. The implementation uses eBPF cgroup programs for network interception and process attribution, plus eBPF programs on Linux Security Module hooks that Meta added for taint propagation.2 As far as the public record shows, no other product in this study tracks taint.

Approvals as capabilities, not suggestions

When Sentinel decides to ask, it creates a pending approval and execution stops. The dialog appears "directly within the client UI — not via their conversation with Muse," and the answer goes straight back to Sentinel.2 That separation matters: an injected instruction in the conversation can't forge an approval, because approvals never pass through the conversation. A grant is "bound to the particular connector/destination and use case," and comes in five shapes. Sentinel chooses which to offer and makes later calls match the granted scope exactly:2

// Inferred shape of a Sentinel grant, reconstructed from the five documented grant types
Grant {
  target:       connector("gmail") | destination("api.example.com")

On the design side, Meta frames the same problem as avoiding "banner blindness — where people approve everything to make it go away." The default allows ordinary browsing and stops for anything hard to undo, and you can make it more or less cautious.3 The approval itself is a structured card with clear accept and reject actions. Meta treats this as one of the places where "conversation isn't enough" and deterministic UI is "non-negotiable."3

The browser

Muse drives a current Chromium "behind a virtualization layer," and you can watch it and take over. The browser is controlled through the broker described above. The sub-agent driving it sees "an accessibility tree snapshot of the page — not the raw DOM," has "no ability to run javascript in the page context, no script verbs, no exec in the browser process," and Chrome DevTools are disabled. While you have control, or while the credential store is filling a form, "the agent is paused and can't act at all."2 Logins typed into the browser go through a separate client UI that routes them straight to authd, so the main agent never sees them. On top of that sits a family of classifiers, each of which blocks the action or prompts for review:2

  • personal data leaving through the browser that isn't related to the task;
  • prompt injection in the page's DOM;
  • prompt injection in images or media on the page;
  • prompt injection in files the browser downloads;
  • attempts to submit high-risk forms.

The VM also checks Meta's existing list of known malicious sites, so the browser won't navigate to them.2

Purchases

Shopping is "one of the most popular browser use cases," and it gets the strictest handling. On sites where your card is already on file, Muse detects the checkout page and asks for approval of the exact purchase every time. Elsewhere it pays from a built-in wallet. At launch that means Stripe's Link, which issues a single-use card number "tied to that particular merchant, a particular dollar amount, and only valid for a limited period of time."2 Meta says Muse is the first agent covered by Link's purchase protections, and Shop Pay and 1Password are "coming soon."1

Account-recovery material is filtered out

An email inbox is a master key: one-time codes and "forgot password" links can take over most of your other accounts. Muse's email connector strips one-time tokens, password-reset links and login magic links using "both deterministic filters and a classifier model."2 The bug bounty shows why both layers are needed: a researcher found that some formatting slipped past the code redaction, as one part of an end-to-end attack that earned $90,000.5

Defense in depth against prompt injection

Meta lists four layers above the deterministic boundaries:2

  1. The model. Muse Spark is trained to resist injection, and Meta calls version 1.3 "close to SOTA" on its own evaluations.
  2. The harness. Anything from an external source is labeled as untrusted input when it enters context.
  3. Classifiers. An ensemble of injection detectors, trained separately from the model, runs in parallel on all external data entering context.
  4. The human. Actions that move data out of the VM need your approval.

Beneath those layers, "deterministic boundaries apply even if Muse is persuaded to behave badly."2 The bounty record shows this working in practice. One attack page imitated the status messages of Muse's own browsing tool and got actions through that should have been caught. Meta added detection signals, changed how suspicious tool output is handled, and reports that the classifiers now flag the original attack "and 100 variations of it."5

Memory and data

Memory is a set of files in your VM, starting with MEMORY.md, which you can read, edit and download. Forgetting is a skill: Muse searches its memories and supporting files for a person, topic or matter and removes what it finds "to the best of its ability."4 The VM is backed up continuously. Conversations and VM data aren't shared with Meta's ad systems, but Meta is candid that Muse's browsing "will appear as your activity," so visiting a shop can still shape the ads you see on Instagram. Trajectories are stripped of key personal identifiers and used for training unless you switch that off.2

Models behind Muse

Muse launched on Muse Spark 1.3, trained for "zero-shot tool calling using CLIs and skills, long context, long-trajectory instruction following with inherent awareness of prompt injection, and multi-agent coordination."2 Against version 1.2, Meta engineers measured "~20% fewer tool calls and ~25% fewer tokens" on coding work.8 On October 12 Meta introduced Muse Wonder, trained at its gigawatt-scale Prometheus data center. It is built around two ideas: "Active Memory" (carrying context about you and pulling it back when it matters) and "Active Focus" (working through blockers on long tasks).9 Since November 6, Muse has been rolling out specialized models trained using Wonder. On Meta's internal evaluations they show "a 2.4x reduction in forgetting critical information" and a 67 percent improvement on everyday tasks where unexpected issues come up.10 All of these are Meta's own benchmarks, without independent replication.

Muse's realtime voice and avatar stack is the most detailed serving design any of the three companies has published. A diffusion transformer conditioned on the stream of speech tokens generates video in causal chunks. It is distilled from 120 model evaluations per chunk to 2, and streams 448×768 video at 25 frames per second, about 870 ms from the end of your turn, with 12 concurrent sessions per GB200 and an invisible watermark.11

Poppy: the business side of the agent

On September 20, Amazon began blocking Muse. Its complaint was that Meta "didn't tell Amazon that Muse would access its store," that "the agent doesn't identify itself when it browses," and that it appeared to store customer credentials.14 Reported On November 13, Meta published version 1.0 of the Personal Agent Protocol ("Poppy"). It is an open standard for how agents work with businesses, developed with partners including Shopify, Stripe, Walmart, Sierra and Decagon.6 A business publishes a poppy.json file declaring what it offers and how to reach it: website, APIs, an MCP server, or its own agent. Poppy then lets the business:6

  • recognize a personal agent acting for a real customer, instead of treating it as a bot;
  • send the agent down the best path for each task;
  • see website visits, API calls and agent conversations as one session;
  • let customers grant scoped access through OAuth, with no password handed to the agent;
  • let its own support agent answer directly, or take over the conversation.

Meta's post doesn't mention Amazon, but the sequence is instructive: an agent that browses as you will eventually be stopped by sites that want to know who is visiting, and the durable fix is a protocol for identifying the agent.

Confidential VM, promised but not shipped

Meta is unusually explicit about the current trust boundary. Today's architecture "does not prevent Meta from accessing data when necessary to support, secure or operate the service." Muse Confidential VM is meant to "cryptographically and verifiably prevent Meta from accessing data in your VM," with the source code given to external auditors and an audit anyone can inspect. Meta said it would arrive "later this year."2 As of November 25, 2026, I found no announcement that it has launched.

5.3 Limits and open issues

  • Prompt injection is still open. Meta's own conclusion is that "Muse isn't immune to attack."2 Its bounty pays up to $300,000 per report, including up to $130,000 for an injection that affects one user. By November 2 it had received more than 630 submissions, found 64 valid issues and paid out about $1.2 million.5
  • The largest bug was in the plumbing, not the model. Four months before launch, an authentication check trusted a header added by a local HTTPS proxy. After a migration added a second proxy layer, outside requests could carry that header too, which exposed conversations and tokens in pre-release employee VMs. Meta paid $300,000 and "continued by removing all trust in the local machine."5 The lesson: trusting the local network quietly breaks when the topology changes.
  • Client apps are part of the attack surface. A malicious Android app could push a link into a privileged web view whose link handler didn't validate the host ($50,000). On the Mac, the auto-updater allowed privilege escalation ($1,000).5
  • Third-party gatekeepers. Amazon's block shows that browsing as the user without identifying as an agent works only until large merchants object.14
  • Consumer only. No enterprise controls, data residency or compliance export is documented.
  • Trust in the operator. TechCrunch framed the launch as "a major test of whether people still trust Meta with their data," a test Confidential VM is meant to help pass.13

What to take from Muse.Put the agent in a box that assumes it is compromised, and move everything that holds a secret or makes a decision outside the box. Track which processes have touched private data, and spend the user's attention only on those. Deliver approvals over a channel the conversation can't reach.

6OpenAI dots: judgment layered on a sandbox

OpenAI's bet is that an aligned model, a second model reviewing its consequential actions, and live monitoring can carry most of the safety load, so you set your intent once and are rarely interrupted. The sandbox contains mistakes; the reviewer decides what crosses out of it.

OpenAI describes dots as "always-on agents built to handle everything," a way of working "that gets to know what matters to you, is always working on your behalf, and takes important work off your plate."16 The clearest way to see what a dot is comes from OpenAI's own positioning against its other products. Chat answers. ChatGPT Work takes a task you delegate. Codex builds software. A dot is "an agent to keep track of an ongoing responsibility, figure out next steps as things change, and follow up between conversations," and it can hand pieces of that work to Work or Codex.18 In other words, a dot is a coordinator of responsibilities sitting on top of products OpenAI already runs.

6.1 High-level design

OpenAI's enterprise documentation describes the architecture in words, and the diagram below follows it. You send requests to the dot and it hands work back. It sends selected context and tasks to delegated Work or Codex threads. It sends any action that needs review to Auto-review, which returns either "a block and reason" or permission to act in the cloud workspace. That workspace connects to your apps and, "through a local-access gate and device permissions," to your own computer.23

6.2 Low-level design

The task fabric

A dot keeps several responsibilities going in one conversation. It "can decide when to pause and wake up to continue work," so routine follow-ups need no fixed schedule. It runs background agents in parallel and can open separate, visible cloud threads.19 For coding work it creates Codex tasks, either in a Codex cloud environment you've already set up or on a computer you've connected. Each delegated task "has its own conversation; it doesn't automatically receive every conversation you've had with your dot."19 In other words, delegation passes context on a need-to-know basis.

Stopping work is just as deliberate. Pause stops the dot's current main task but not its delegated tasks or future scheduled runs. Each of those is stopped separately, and "stopping work doesn't undo completed actions."20 If you build something similar, copy this: separate controls for each kind of work make it obvious what is still running.

The execution environment

Each dot has its own cloud computer for browsing, analysis, files and tools. Within it, "sandboxing restricts what code and tools that dot can access." Each user's environment is isolated from everyone else's, and OpenAI maintains the Linux system and the Chrome browser.17 The important part is the next sentence: dots "run code in environments that are separate from the systems that coordinate their work and enforce key safeguards," so "they cannot use that access to change those safety systems or turn off required checks."17 Dots reuse the cloud-computer infrastructure of ChatGPT Work and can keep snapshots across sessions. In workspaces with data residency or Enterprise Key Management those snapshots are short-lived; elsewhere they can last as long as the dot.23 Admins switch four capabilities independently: Use a browser, Allow network access (code and shell), Allow computer use and Use password manager.23

Built-in action rules

Before any reviewer gets involved, dots follow fixed rules that sort every action into one of three outcomes: proceed, confirm, or hand back.17

  • Confirm every time: permanently deleting data, installing or running software from an unrecognized source, or granting new security-sensitive access.
  • Hand back to you: changing a password, or moving money between financial accounts. The dot helps with everything around the step but never takes the step itself.
  • Authorize by sensitivity: to send a message or share a file, the dot needs authorization that covers both the information and the kind of recipient. "Health data will always require the user to specify a named recipient," contact details need a class of recipient ("any airline company"), and a Custom Rule can widen the rules for less sensitive data.

Authorization "stays tied to your instructions for the task; continuing later or delegating work does not expand it."17 An approval granted for one task can't be reused for a different one.

Identity: the Bot is you

"A Bot has no identity or credentials of its own." It acts as the signed-in member, so it "can never hold more access than the person it belongs to," every action is attributable to a named person, and there is "no separate machine identity" to provision or rotate. The one exception is team-managed connectors, which may use team or service-account credentials.39 Inside the computer's browser, people sign in through their company's identity provider, much like enrolling a new laptop, and Okta Verify keys stored in the computer's keyring survive when the computer is rebuilt.3936 Compared with dots, which plan specialist dots with "their own identity for access management,"16 this is the opposite choice: simpler to audit, but impossible to scope a Bot below its owner's access.

Connectors and MCP

"OAuth tokens are held on Cursor's connector backend, and the Bot invokes tools without receiving them."38 Apps are account-wide: every Bot you run can use every account you connect. You can add an MCP server by URL (Remote HTTPS, with optional headers and OAuth client) or as a command that runs on the computer. A command server becomes available to your other Bots too, and adding one on a private network or with a private certificate authority needs your approval.38 The documentation's table of hosts reveals some of the plumbing: QuickBooks, Workday, Zoho and WhatsApp Business connect through ah-api.merge.dev, a unified-API vendor, and Gmail can be reached through Google's hosted MCP endpoints.39 A marketplace Bot called tinkabot wraps any API in a plugin, with an MCP server and skills, tests it, and publishes it only when you say so.37

Secrets and the Vault

To sign in, a Bot tries three sources in order: Vault, then 1Password, then a Secure Form that asks you. Whichever it uses, "the login goes straight into the page, and your Bot never sees it." API keys go through a masked "secure secret card."37 The fine print matters. The Bot "can see which logins are saved and the member's saved contact info," and "in chats in the Grok Bot app, it can also see the number and security code of a card the member saved in Vault, so it can type them at checkout."39 Card numbers are scrubbed from memories, including when hidden characters split them, and a Bot refuses to send a file containing a saved secret.36 Vault isn't available on Team Bots.39

Auto Review, rules and approval cards

Auto Review is "an independent review model that evaluates risky Bot actions before they run, covering shell commands, app calls, computer use, automation writes (changes to routines and event triggers), and delegation such as Cloud Agent and subagent launches." It returns one of three outcomes: proceed, require approval, or deny. It is on by default, and members can switch it off unless an Enterprise admin enforces it. Admins can also add team rules that every member inherits as locked rows.39 Members add their own rules in plain language. An Ask first rule always stops a matching action. An Allow automatically rule lets an action through only "when the reviewer finds no other reason to stop," and when rules conflict, Ask first wins.39 Since version 1.2.3, each connector tool can also be set to Always allow, Auto-review, Needs approval or Blocked.36 On an approval card, Deny means the Bot "does not try it again until the member sends another message."39

The changelog shows how much tuning approvals need:36

  • v0.77.0: "lets through more private actions you can undo without an approval card," and approval cards stay open for 30 minutes instead of 10.
  • v0.79.2: when a page hides instructions for AI agents, the Bot tells you it found them and didn't follow them.
  • v1.2.0: command approval cards show the exact command "with hidden characters marked," and prompt links with suspicious hidden characters are blocked.

The documented gap: Auto Review "does not review every side effect. Memory writes and most settings changes are examples."39 Running commands on your own machine is a separate control, with three settings: Ask every time, Always allow, or Never allow. The docs recommend Never allow unless a Bot has a specific reason to touch local files.39

Network policy

Enterprise admins choose one of four modes: allow everything; allow everything except listed sites; Cursor's defaults plus a team allowlist; or the team allowlist only. "Teams without a policy default to allow-all," and self-serve Teams can't set a policy at all.39 The block list takes up to 50 hostnames and is "best effort, not a security boundary": a request to a bare IP address gets through, and web search results aren't filtered.39 Changes reach running computers in about a minute. All customers share the same static egress IPs (no dedicated IPs), and some sites block them as datacenter traffic. A member can route the computer's web traffic through their own desktop instead.3940

Skills and routines

SpaceXSI teaches a clear progression: "Start with a one-time task. Make it reliable, save the method as a skill, and only then automate it."43 A good skill, by their checklist, states six things:

  1. when to use it;
  2. the inputs and access it needs;
  3. the sequence of work;
  4. how to check the result;
  5. what to return;
  6. what requires approval.

Skills form one private library shared by all your Bots, and you can publish one to your team. Teach a task records up to ten minutes of you doing a browser workflow (without microphone audio) and drafts a skill from it.43 A routine assigns one Bot a workflow to run on a schedule or when something happens: a Slack message, a GitHub, Linear, Sentry or PagerDuty event, an email to the Bot's address, or a webhook. "Only new activity counts," and the docs warn against broad listeners like "every new message," which "consume usage, and increase the chance of acting on irrelevant input."43 A routine that loses a connector sign-in skips its runs until you reconnect.36

Memory

Each Bot has its own memory, holding your preferences, the rules you've asked it to keep, facts about you, and "a dated log of what it has worked on with you." A few facts, such as your name and time zone, are shared by all your Bots.45 Since version 1.2.3, a Bot "keeps separate memories for people and organizations, your profile, your standing instructions, and your projects."36 A Bot "decides on its own when something is worth keeping. It doesn't ask first." There is no memory screen: you ask in chat what it remembers, correct it, or tell it to forget.45 The docs carry a line worth adopting outright: "Memory is not a source of truth." Changing facts belong in the source system, and safety boundaries belong in approvals and rules, "rather than memory alone."45

Many Bots, and Bots for teams

Your primary Bot, marked with a star, is your main point of contact. It "checks in with you on its own and coordinates your other Bots," and the advice is to give recurring work with its own sources, schedule or approvals a Bot of its own.37 Bots hand work to each other with asynchronous messages, and group chats hold two to six Bots. In a group, approval cards, login requests and drafts never appear, and the docs advise "a single owner at each stage" because "parallel handoffs create duplicate work."44 Every reply counts toward your usage, including replies in groups and messages between Bots.44

A Team Bot is published from a personal Bot, either as a copy that keeps its chat and memories or as a fresh start. Each teammate gets their own conversation, and the Bot keeps private notes for each person. Their usage counts against their own allowance.36 "What teammates teach a Team Bot waits under Review learnings until the owner or a Manager approves it." That is a moderation queue for shared memory, which none of the other products has.36 For each connector, a Team Bot can use individual accounts, its owner's shared account, or an account of its own.36

Presence: meetings, phones, inboxes, X

Grok Bot goes furthest at putting an agent where people already talk.

  • Meetings. A Bot joins Google Meet, Zoom or Microsoft Teams calls as "Bot name (your first name's Bot) - transcribing," answers in the meeting chat, and sends a summary and action items afterwards. For large or external calls it joins listen-only and asks whether the meeting is trusted, and it won't share your credentials in a meeting.4636
  • Phone calls. In the US, a Bot can call businesses for you. You accept calling terms once and confirm each call. During the call you see a live transcript, can take over, and get questions the Bot can't answer with a countdown to reply. Calls "aren't used to train SpaceXSI's models, whatever your privacy settings."44
  • Email. A Bot can claim its own address; anything it sends from it waits for your approval on a draft card.36
  • X. Tagging @bot under a post hands that post to your main Bot as a task. @bot replies on X only to confirm, and the work stays in the app.48

Observability and the enterprise envelope

Two pipelines, kept separate. Audit logs record admin, security and control-plane events, such as Bot creation, access changes and routine changes. Action Recording captures Bot actions, including scrubbed shell commands, for 90 days. It is off by default and can be exported over OpenTelemetry tagged cursor.surface=grok_bot.39 Computers run in the United States. Customer-chosen models are not supported: "the team model allowlist does not govern Grok Bot." Zero data retention follows Cursor's existing agreements with model providers. On-premises and bring-your-own-image deployment are not supported. Anysphere's ISO/IEC 27001 and 42001 certifications cover Grok Bot.39

7.3 Limits and open issues

  • No isolation between your Bots. A compromised or confused Bot has every sign-in, file and saved card that any of your Bots has. The docs say so plainly: "Your Bots aren't separated from each other."37
  • Payments lean toward convenience. A card saved in Vault fills without per-use approval, its number and security code are visible to the Bot in app chats, and one approval can cover a batch of up to 500 payments for 24 hours.5339
  • Strong controls are Enterprise-only. Network policy, enforced Auto Review, inference hooks, audit logs and Action Recording are all Enterprise features. Self-serve Teams default to allow-all networking.39
  • Review gaps. Memory writes and most settings changes aren't reviewed, and the block list is best effort.39
  • Shared egress identity. All customers share the same static IP ranges, so sites that block datacenter traffic block Grok Bot.40
  • No model choice. The serving mix "can change over time with no fixed vendor set guaranteed," which matters if your contract restricts subprocessors.39

What to take from Grok Bot.Keep OAuth tokens on a backend the agent can't touch, and treat connector calls and web traffic as two egress paths that each need a policy. Let customers bring their own policy server into the model loop. Grow agent skills deliberately (task, then skill, then routine), and put shared team memory behind a review queue.

8Component by component

Sections 5 to 7 took each product apart on its own terms. This section lines them up component by component, says which approach the evidence favors, and turns each comparison into a design rule you can carry into your own build.

8.1 The execution substrate and the unit of isolation

All three give the agent a persistent Linux machine with a browser, a shell and files, because real work (compiling code, filling forms, keeping a session alive overnight) needs one. They differ on what one machine protects. Muse isolates per person and then splits the machine itself into two security domains, so the agent's own box holds no secrets.2 Dots give each dot its own computer and keep the coordination and safety systems on separate infrastructure.17 Grok Bot isolates users with hardware-level separation but deliberately shares one machine among all of a user's Bots.40 Grok Bot's design is the cheapest and the easiest for Bots to collaborate in. It is also the one where a mistake by one Bot reaches every other Bot.

Rule.Make the isolation unit match the unit of trust: one principal and one purpose. Whatever you choose, nothing that holds a secret or makes a safety decision should run inside the box where untrusted content is processed.

8.2 The gatekeeper

The three gatekeepers get their authority from different places. Meta calls Sentinel "a separate host-side agent," but the checks it documents are mostly deterministic: user policy, layer-4 and layer-7 inspection and taint state, with classifiers running in a separate service.2 Auto-review is a model judging intent: it reads a compact transcript and decides whether an action fits what you asked for.25 Grok Bot's Auto Review is also a model, wrapped in admin-enforced rules, with customers' own policy servers available through inference hooks.3942 OpenAI names the trade-off itself: compared with traditional security systems, Auto-review "sacrifices determinism for expressivity."26 A deterministic gate can't tell that an email to a new address is fine because you asked for it. A model gate can be talked into things. The answer is to layer both, and every product here does so to some degree.

Rule.Use deterministic controls for where data may go and which secret may be used, a reviewer model for whether this action fits the user's intent, and a human for anything you can't undo. Run the gatekeeper outside anything the agent can modify.

8.3 Credentials and secrets

A spectrum runs from strongest to weakest. Muse's surrogate tokens mean that even code the agent writes never holds a real secret; the real one is swapped in "at the network boundary."2 Dots and Grok Bot keep app tokens on a backend and fill website passwords by reference, so the model never sees them.1739 The weak point is the same everywhere: once a site is signed in, its session cookie is a credential sitting in the agent's browser. OpenAI says code on the computer can read exposed tokens and cookies, and Grok Bot shares sessions across all your Bots.2338 Muse's email connector adds a step the others don't document: it strips one-time codes and reset links, so an inbox can't be turned into a master key.2

Rule.Secrets never enter the agent's process: inject them at the boundary, after authorization. Treat session cookies and one-time codes as credentials too, and keep account-recovery material out of anything the agent reads.

8.4 Browser automation

The browser is where untrusted content and the agent's actions meet most often. Muse's browsing sub-agent sees an accessibility-tree snapshot instead of the raw DOM, can't run JavaScript, and is paused whenever a human or the credential filler is in control.2 Dots and Grok Bot both offer live viewing and take-over, and both have learned to prefer structured connectors over clicking. As of version 1.2.3, "for a site that has a connector, the Bot uses the connector, or shows its connect card, before opening the browser."36 Grok Bot's changelog also shows how much ordinary engineering browser automation still needs: clicks that drift, hung keystrokes that time out after 30 seconds, CAPTCHA and QR-code handoffs.36

Rule.Use an API when one exists and the browser when it doesn't. Give the browsing agent the narrowest useful view of the page, never a script console. Pause the agent whenever a human or the credential filler is in control.

8.5 Payments

The safest documented design is Muse's: checkout detection, approval of the exact purchase every time, and a single-use card valid for one merchant, one amount and a short time, so a leaked card is worthless.2 Dots add a hardware factor: you approve each Wallet payment with biometrics, even under a standing instruction.23 Grok Bot optimizes for throughput instead: saved cards, batch order cards, and Deal Watcher and Gift Concierge templates that buy "only the gifts you approve."5336

Rule.Bind every payment credential to a merchant, an amount and an expiry, and approve each transaction outside the conversation, with the exact details shown. Never use a reusable card number the model can read.

8.6 Memory

Memory is where the products differ most on user control. Muse stores memory as files you can open, edit and download.4 Grok Bot sorts memory into typed stores (people and organizations, profile, standing instructions, projects), shares a few facts across Bots, and puts team learnings behind a review queue, but you can only inspect it by asking in chat.3645 Dots keep their notes separate from ChatGPT memory, and in enterprise settings a single memory can't be deleted without deleting the whole dot.23 On the modeling side, Meta's specialized models are trained to "recognize what type of information is temporary versus permanent" and to tell "what is still relevant" from "what has become stale."10 That is the right goal, and it is still graded only by Meta's own evaluations.

Rule.Store memory as typed records with provenance and an expected lifetime, and let people view, edit and delete each record. Never store secrets in memory. Treat memory as a hint, re-checking against the source of truth before anything consequential.

8.7 Proactivity and time

Each product separates noticing from doing, with a different degree of rigor. Dots enforce the split in code: background research tools are read-only.17 Grok Bot enforces it in the interaction: the primary Bot offers, and you decide.47 Muse enforces it in surfacing: when background work finishes, Muse "evaluates whether the result is worth surfacing" before it notifies you.3 Scheduling has converged too: an agent that decides its own wake-ups for routine follow-ups, explicit schedules with time zones and end dates, and narrow event triggers.1943

Rule.Run proactive work in read-only mode, enforced by the tools rather than the prompt. Turn findings into suggestions, and require an explicit grant for any write. Hold notifications to a "worth the interruption" bar, and give people a dial for it.

8.8 Multi-agent delegation

Delegation can widen what an agent is allowed to do if you're not careful. OpenAI sets the right invariant: authorization "stays tied to your instructions for the task; continuing later or delegating work does not expand it," and delegated tasks get selected context, not everything.1719 Grok Bot offers the richest peer model, with asynchronous handoffs, group chats and Team Bots, and pairs it with practical advice: "a single owner at each stage."44 Its cost model is a useful warning: every message between Bots consumes usage.44

Rule.Delegation narrows authority, never widens it: pass the minimum context and the parent's grants, scoped down. Give each stage one owner, and make the cost of agent-to-agent chatter visible.

8.9 Channels and presence

Distribution decides more than features do. Muse lives in WhatsApp, dots inside ChatGPT, Slack, Teams and text messages, and Grok Bot in Slack, Teams, meetings, phone calls, email and X.1162234 The patterns that keep this safe repeat across products:

  • one agent identity and one memory across channels;
  • a check against the audience before anything is posted to a shared space;1936
  • approvals that always send you back to a trusted surface, such as a texted link that opens the request in ChatGPT;22
  • command authority limited to the owner, even in shared channels.23

Rule.Being able to use a fact is not permission to share it. Check every outbound message against everyone who will read it. Deliver approvals only on a surface you control.

8.10 Governance and observability

Every product shows the user a trail of activity. The enterprise products go further. Dots export prompts, replies, tool calls and background activity through the Compliance API.23 Grok Bot keeps control-plane audit logs separate from Action Recording, and exports both over OpenTelemetry.39 OpenAI's guide makes an important point: a tool record shows only what the agent requested. To know what actually happened, check the destination system or a proxy log.23

Rule.Log three things separately: what the agent proposed, who or what approved it, and what effect the outside world confirms. Keep those logs somewhere the agent can't write to.

8.11 Models

All three route work across several models, and none of them treats the model as the security boundary.91639 What they train for is more useful to copy: zero-shot tool use through CLIs and skills, following instructions over long trajectories, awareness of prompt injection, and calibration about irreversible actions.28 OpenAI's "blocker deception" evaluation measures whether the model honestly reports what stopped it, and GPT-6.1 Terra failed to do so in about 8 percent of the tasks chosen to elicit that failure.23 For an agent that works while you sleep, honest failure reports may matter more than raw capability.

Rule.Keep the reviewer model separate from the acting model. Evaluate both for honesty about failure, not just task success.

9Blueprint for your own agent

If you are building something in this space, here is the reference design I would start from. It takes the strongest documented choice for each component. Every box is something one of the three products already ships, and the arrangement is Inferred: my synthesis, not anyone's published design.

Build it in this order

The order follows a simple principle: each phase removes a reason to fear the next one.

  1. Foundations. A per-user sandbox, one agent harness, and a few connectors whose tokens live on a backend, read-only at first. Drafts instead of sends. Approval cards in UI the conversation can't reach. An append-only audit log. All three products start here.
  2. The boundary. An egress proxy with an allowlist and SSRF checks; surrogate tokens and a credential broker; a browser behind a broker that gives the agent a reduced view; a rules engine with confirm every time and hand back classes; and typed, scoped grants.217
  3. Time. A scheduler, self-chosen wake-ups and narrow event triggers; read-only proactive research; a high bar for notifications; and a work ledger with a separate stop control for each kind of work.192043
  4. Judgment. A reviewer model for escalations. Start it in shadow mode, as Grok Bot does with inference hooks, then enforce. Add a circuit breaker on repeated denials, and taint tracking to cut unnecessary prompts. Build an evaluation suite covering overeager actions, prompt injection and honest blocker reporting.26422
  5. Scale out. Delegation that passes minimal context; more channels, with audience checks; payments through bound single-use cards; identifying the agent to the sites it visits; team agents with a review queue for what they learn; and enterprise hooks, residency and compliance export.63623

10Open questions and what to watch

  • Does Muse Confidential VM ship, and does the audit hold up? It is the only plan here that would stop the operator itself from reading your agent's data. Meta promised it for 2026.2
  • Does anyone other than Meta adopt Poppy? A protocol that only one agent speaks is a partner program, not a standard. The partner list (Shopify, Stripe, Walmart and others) is a strong start.6
  • Do dots close their review-coverage gaps? Watch for Auto-review coverage of browser and desktop actions, more than one dot per user, and specialist dots with their own identities, including in Microsoft Agent 365.2316
  • Does Grok Bot add isolation between Bots, or network controls below the Enterprise tier? Today both are documented as absent.3839
  • Do merchants open up or close off? Amazon's block of Muse is the leading indicator of whether agents can shop on the open web, or only through protocols and partnerships.14
